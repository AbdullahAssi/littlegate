# Harden first, then out-design Android's free controls

Littlegate's biggest opportunity is not a long feature list. It is to be the one parental-control app that **stays on, can't be quietly bypassed, and treats a shared phone as the main use case**, all with no account. The first things to add, folded into the current phases 0–8 before release, are: a protection health check that **fails closed**; a per-OEM battery wizard; **per-app daily limits** with explicit "always blocked" and "blocked on schedule" states; schedule timers that ignore clock changes; a never-block list for the dialer and emergency calls; a kid-facing "why was this blocked / ask a grown-up" screen approved with the PIN on the same phone; one-tap blocks for named services; and a dedicated **AI chatbot category**. The urgency comes from two platform moves. **Android 16 now ships free, PIN-protected parental controls** with daily caps, downtime, per-app limits and Chrome/Search filtering ([android.com](https://www.android.com/articles/get-started-with-android-parental-controls/)). That means any Littlegate free tier without per-app limits and schedules is worse than the OS itself. Second, **Android 17's Advanced Protection reportedly revokes accessibility from apps that aren't accessibility tools** ([Android Authority](https://www.androidauthority.com/android-17-beta-2-advanced-protection-mode-accessibility-apps-3648860)). That would let a child switch off Littlegate's app guard. The research also found problems in the current plan. Using Device Admin to block uninstalling runs into Play's ransomware rule. `USE_EXACT_ALARM` and `QUERY_ALL_PACKAGES` would very likely be rejected. And a DNS-only tunnel can be bypassed by strict-mode Private DNS. Location, cloud dashboards, message scanning and screen capture should be left out on purpose, as stated positions. The recommended business model: a generous free core, a **one-time "Plus" unlock of about $19.99–$29.99**, and later an optional low-cost subscription that only covers things that genuinely cost money to run.

A note on how the tags work. Each idea in the table below carries four tags. **Privacy fit**: LOCAL means nothing leaves the phone except blocklist downloads; BACKEND means it needs a server. **Google Play policy risk**: Low, Med or High. **Native Kotlin effort**, for one experienced developer: S is under 1 week, M is 1–3 weeks, L is more than 3 weeks. **Roadmap phase**. The risk and effort tags are researcher estimates, not measurements. Items marked "device-validate" depend on behaviour the research could not confirm. Phase numbers follow the table in PLAN §7 (0 Setup … 5 Launcher + guard, 6 Schedule + boot, 7 Lock mode, 8 Polish + release). Elsewhere in PLAN (§1 and the §3.1 onboarding list), Lock mode is called "phase 6", which contradicts that table and should be fixed. Work after release is grouped as v1.1 and v2.

## Silent failure and bypasses outrank every new "feature"

Parents' most damaging complaint is that controls **stop working without telling anyone**. Accessibility-based blockers are routinely killed by Samsung, Xiaomi and Huawei battery management "even when everything looks correctly set up," and they drop after reboots or when battery saver turns on ([AppBlock](https://appblock.app/why-do-some-device-manufacturers-break-accessibility-or-background-blocking/); [Samsung community](https://r1.community.samsung.com/t5/galaxy-s/accessibility-keeps-turning-itself-off/td-p/22542025)). Qustodio's own help page admits that "if accessibility is turned OFF, all monitoring will be disabled" ([Qustodio Help](https://help.qustodio.com/hc/en-us/articles/360005219838-Why-and-how-does-Qustodio-use-Android-s-Accessibility-setting)). PLAN already logs `onRevoke()` and accessibility loss as tamper events. That is reactive bookkeeping. The missing piece is **fail-closed behaviour**: if the VPN or the guard dies, the Kid Mode launcher locks behind the PIN instead of falling open. It should come with a live status card ("VPN on · Guard on · Battery unrestricted · Autostart OK") and a per-OEM wizard that deep-links to Samsung's "Never sleeping apps", Xiaomi's Autostart and similar settings, and re-runs after OS updates.

The bypass catalogue is well documented. Bitdefender's 2025 list of Family Link bypasses is effectively a test plan for Littlegate. It includes:

- ADB tools such as "Chronolink"
- Help and Terms links that open hidden WebView browsers
- Dual Apps and app clones
- Samsung Secure Folder and Android Private Space
- DeX, the Emergency Dialer and the Accessibility Menu
- Changing the clock or time zone, and rebooting
- Links sent through "always allowed" messaging apps
- Reopening apps from Recents

([Bitdefender](https://www.bitdefender.com/en-us/blog/hotforsecurity/family-link-bypass-android-2025)). Bitdefender's countermeasures are **manual parent checklists**. Turning them into automatic checks is a concrete, marketable differentiator. On a shared phone the bypasses that matter most are in-app browsers, Recents, the Accessibility Menu, competing VPNs and clock changes. Factory reset, account removal and Secure Folder mainly matter for the child's-own-phone Lock mode. One complaint shows the opposite failure. In May 2026 a Family Link bug blocked **outgoing calls** on supervised phones, and the workaround was unavailable for accounts under 13 ([Android Authority](https://www.androidauthority.com/google-family-link-bug-3663231/); [Android Headlines](https://www.androidheadlines.com/2026/05/google-family-link-bug-blocks-outgoing-calls-leaving-families-frustrated.html)). That argues for a hard-coded never-block list (dialer, emergency calls, parent contacts) that no setting can override.

Kids' complaints point the same way, from the other side. In 736 Play reviews of 37 safety apps, **76% of child reviewers gave one star**. They called the apps restrictive, invasive and harmful to their relationship with their parents ([Ghosh et al., CHI 2018](https://epublications.marquette.edu/mscs_fac/618)). A feature analysis of 75 Android apps found the features heavily favoured parental restriction and monitoring over teen self-regulation ([Wisniewski et al., CSCW 2017](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/parental-control-vs-teen-self-regulation-is-there-a-middle-ground/)). Parent-set limits combined with ongoing discussion are linked to the best outcomes ([syndicated APA coverage](https://newschannel9.com/news/nation-world/american-psychological-association-issues-recommendations-around-kids-social-media-use-digital-citizenship-literacy-teens-youth-families-tiktok-instagram-snap-facebook-youtube-mental-health)). The evidence is correlational. Still, it justifies three cheap features with high trust value: a kid-facing "why was this blocked" screen, an ask-for-more-time flow, and an **explicit no-stealth stance**.

Three ideas belong in a later lane, not the P0 core. **Call/SMS contact allowlists** need the Device Owner plus the dialer role and are large in scope (L, Med–High risk). **Battery transparency** depends on data that may not be available to the app. **Content packs** (curated allowlists such as "Pre-K learning") are cheap to host like blocklists but need editorial work. Blocking newly installed apps needs no separate feature, because the allowlist model already makes new apps unusable in Kid Mode.

## Seven planned items the research says to change

Several decisions in PLAN conflict with current Play policy or platform behaviour. Each is cheaper to fix now than after a rejection.

**Anti-uninstall via Device Admin (PLAN §5.6).** Play's Ransomware section names "using device admin features to block removal" as an example. It asks apps to provide "a clear way to remove the app," and "Elevated Privilege Abuse" separately covers preventing uninstallation ([Play: Device Abuse](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9888380)). Legacy device-admin policies have also been deprecated since Android Q ([DevicePolicyManager](https://developer.android.com/reference/android/app/admin/DevicePolicyManager)). Drop the Device Admin tier. Use `setUninstallBlocked` only under Device Owner. For non-DO use, copy the Qustodio/Kidslox pattern of a **PIN gate on the uninstall screen** ([Kidslox](https://kidslox.com/guide-to/how-does-kidslox-work/)), and document a parent removal path.

**`USE_EXACT_ALARM` "if allowed" (§5.5).** It is not allowed. That permission is limited to alarm/timer and calendar apps ([Play: sensitive permissions](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16558241)). `SCHEDULE_EXACT_ALARM` has been denied by default since Android 14 ([Android 14](https://developer.android.com/about/versions/14/changes/schedule-exact-alarms)). Add a grant flow and an inexact `setWindow`/WorkManager fallback.

**`QUERY_ALL_PACKAGES` (§9).** Don't declare it. Parental control isn't a named use, and the policy expects a less intrusive method ([Play: QUERY_ALL_PACKAGES](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/10158779?hl=en)). A `<queries>` element for launcher intents covers the allowlist picker.

**UsageStats as a mere fallback (§5.4).** Advanced Protection makes a guard that doesn't depend on accessibility **a primary path**. It is not enough to just poll usage. VpnService and foreground services are **not** exempt from Android's limits on starting activities from the background. User-granted `SYSTEM_ALERT_WINDOW` is exempt, so the guard needs that overlay permission to bounce the child out of an app or cover it ([Android: background starts](https://developer.android.com/guide/components/activities/background-starts)). Whether the HOME-role launcher can bring itself to the front programmatically is unverified and needs a device test. Detect the mode itself with `AdvancedProtectionManager` ([reference](https://developer.android.com/reference/android/security/advancedprotection/AdvancedProtectionManager)), alert the parent, and lock Kid Mode.

**Plaintext UDP/53 upstream and a DNS-only route (§5.1).** Play requires VPN apps to "encrypt the data from the device to the VPN tunnel endpoint" ([Play: VpnService](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/12564964?hl=en)). Sending upstream queries over DoH or DoT answers any reviewer question and fits the privacy brand. Separately, routing only the virtual DNS IP means strict-mode Private DNS (DoT on port 853) to a third-party resolver never enters the tunnel. That is an inference to test on devices. Google's own docs warn that system traffic to a Private DNS resolver "may not go through the VPN" ([DevicePolicyManager](https://developer.android.com/reference/android/app/admin/DevicePolicyManager)). The fix is to route known resolver IPs and port 853 into the tunnel and drop them, without routing all traffic.

**"Distribute Lock mode outside Play" as a risk fallback (§10).** This route is closing. Developer verification has been enforced in Brazil, Indonesia, Singapore and Thailand since 2026-09-30 and goes global in 2027 ([Android developer verification](https://developer.android.com/developer-verification/guides?authuser=3)). Sideloaded apps also hit "restricted settings" for Accessibility, Usage access, Device admin and overlays ([Android Authority](https://androidauthority.com/android-15-restricted-settings-sideloading-3481098)). Play is effectively the only route.

**Smaller decisions to settle.** Leave `isMonitoringTool` undeclared only while nothing leaves the device. Declaring it triggers Play Protect warnings ([Play: isMonitoringTool](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/12955211?hl=en)), and any remote dashboard would bring every monitoring-app (stalkerware) requirement into force. For any extra foreground service, prefer the `systemExempted` type, which a VPN app or a holder of `SCHEDULE_EXACT_ALARM` can use, over a `specialUse` declaration that Play reviews by hand ([Android: FGS types](https://developer.android.com/develop/background-work/services/fgs/service-types)). Keep the accessibility guard strictly rule-based ("if X, do Y"). Play explicitly permits that, and it bans autonomous decision-making ([Play: Accessibility](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/10964491)). Finally, give the per-domain `blocked_event` log the retention dial (#13), and show aggregate stats rather than browsing history for teens.

## Platform threats: free OS controls, Advanced Protection and DNS leaks

**Android 16's built-in Parental Controls** sit in Settings behind a PIN. They cover a daily screen-time cap, nightly downtime, per-app limits or blocks, and mature-content filters for Chrome and Google Search. Google's article is dated 2026-03-16 ([android.com](https://www.android.com/articles/get-started-with-android-parental-controls/)), while Internet Matters dates the release to December 2025 ([Internet Matters](https://www.internetmatters.org/parental-controls/smartphones-and-other-devices/android-smartphone/)). These controls run through a privileged GMS supervision package that third parties can't use ([Bayton](https://sysapps.bayton.org/packages/com.google.android.gms.supervision)), so Littlegate can't beat them on integration. It has to win on six things:

- DNS category filtering beyond Chrome and Search
- YouTube Restricted Mode
- The kid launcher for a shared phone
- Bypass hardening
- No Google account
- Support for phones older than Android 16

**Advanced Protection on Android 17** is the most serious threat. Reports from Beta 2 say it limits accessibility to verified `isAccessibilityTool` apps, **automatically revokes it from installed apps**, and names monitoring apps and launchers as losing access ([Android Authority](https://www.androidauthority.com/android-17-beta-2-advanced-protection-mode-accessibility-apps-3648860); [The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/03/android-17-blocks-non-accessibility.html)). Littlegate cannot claim the accessibility-tool flag ([Play: Accessibility](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/10964491)). The evidence is **secondary only**, because Google's primary announcement wasn't reachable, so the final behaviour needs confirming. Plan for the worst case anyway. An older child could turn on Advanced Protection and silently strip the guard. Qustodio, Kidslox and Littlegate all share this weakness, so a solid overlay-based guard (with Settings kept off the allowlist) and the Device Owner path become **competitive advantages**.

**DNS leaks** remain structural. Chrome's automatic Secure DNS upgrades only to the same provider, but **manual configuration lets a user pick any DoH provider with no fallback**. Chrome turns Secure DNS off when enterprise policies are present ([Chromium Blog](https://blog.chromium.org/2020/09/a-safer-and-more-private-browsing.html)). Without a Device Owner, Littlegate can only block known DoH and DoT endpoints and detect Private DNS. `DISALLOW_CONFIG_PRIVATE_DNS` is available only to a Device Owner ([UserManager](https://developer.android.com/reference/android/os/UserManager)). A competing VPN is easier to catch, because Android allows one active VpnService and Littlegate receives `onRevoke()`. Fail-closed turns that signal into enforcement. Android 17's default ECH hides TLS SNI but not DNS queries ([Android 17 changes](https://developer.android.com/about/versions/17/behavior-changes-17?hl=en)), so it doesn't affect the DNS filter. It does rule out adding SNI-based filtering later.

**Private Space** is the main weakness of shared-phone Kid Mode. Its apps are hidden from Recents, notifications and other apps while it is locked ([Android 15 features](https://developer.android.com/about/versions/15/features#private-space)). Without a Device Owner, Littlegate can't stop a Private Space from being created, while Device Owner devices block one by default ([UserManager](https://developer.android.com/reference/android/os/UserManager)). Whether Littlegate's VPN and usage tracking can see Private Space apps is unverified. In Kid Mode the practical defence is that the launcher never exposes the Private Space container. Lock mode closes the gap fully.

## Ideas to reject outright, and why

Each rejected idea fails at least one of these tests: it needs a server, it conflicts with the privacy promise, it carries high Play risk, or the platform blocks it.

- **Location, geofencing and drive safety** need a backend and clash with the privacy stance. Microsoft retired exactly these features in November 2024 because subscribers used digital-safety features far more ([Microsoft](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/help/4293682)).
- **A cloud parent dashboard** would bring every monitoring-app (stalkerware) requirement into force once child data is sent off the device ([Play](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9888380)). It would also recreate the failures parents complain about: Family Link access codes rejected while the device was online, and approval requests that never arrived ([Sikayetvar](https://www.sikayetvar.com/en/family-link-us/google-family-link-locked-my-childs-phone-and-wont-accept-the-access-code)).
- **AI chat scanning through accessibility** is technically feasible but carries the highest risk of anything assessed. It conflicts with PLAN's no-content-filtering rule, and it stops working under Advanced Protection anyway. Vendors that do offer chatbot controls, such as OpenAI and Meta, deliberately show parents no transcripts ([Meta](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/10/teen-ai-safety-approach/)).
- **On-device AI analysis of other apps** fails on two routes. **Gemini Nano** returns `BACKGROUND_USE_BLOCKED` unless the calling app is in the foreground, so it can't watch other apps ([ML Kit GenAI](https://developers.google.com/ml-kit/genai)). A bundled **nudity classifier on screen captures** would need MediaProjection consent or accessibility screenshots, with battery cost and bad optics (High risk, L effort).
- **Screenshots, call/SMS logs and SMS commands** are surveillance, and SMS permissions are likely blocked by Play policy.
- **Stealth or hidden-icon modes** are banned outright ([Play](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9888380)).
- **Bedtime grayscale** has no public API. It needs an adb-granted `WRITE_SECURE_SETTINGS`, which can't ship; an overlay tint is the most that's possible.
- **A work-profile architecture** is a poor fit, because the child simply uses the personal side of the phone.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question. It is no longer "what should Littlegate add?" but "**what would a free OS feature and one Android 17 setting take away?**" Per-app limits and schedules are now the price of entry, not premium features. The accessibility guard has to be treated as one enforcement path among several, not the foundation. The defensible ground is narrow but real: **a shared phone where siblings each get a profile, a guard that fails closed, and approvals that work offline**. Offline signed unlock codes are the strongest new idea, because they give Lock mode remote-style approval with no server. The research found no mainstream app doing this; only Family Link's clock-dependent code exists ([Google](https://support.google.com/families/answer/7103340?hl=en)).

On business model, every competitor sells annual subscriptions of about $50–$100 per family. Qustodio is about $55–60 a year and Bark Premium is $99 a year ([Battencyber](https://battencyber.com/briefs/best-bark-alternatives/)), and the research found no mainstream lifetime licence. Littlegate's core costs nothing to run per user, so the clearest proposition is a three-tier model. The **free tier** should match Android 16 and beat it on privacy: filtering, named-service and AI blocks, per-app limits, schedules, one profile, health check. A **one-time Plus unlock of about $19.99–$29.99** would add multiple profiles, toddler modes, the earned-time wallet, the agreement builder, longer history and offline unlock codes. An **optional subscription** would follow later, anchored near NextDNS's $19.90 a year ([Control D](https://controld.com/blog/nextdns-pricing/)), and should cover only the E2EE relay and curated content packs. The free tier deliberately includes safety features (AI-companion blocking and per-app limits), because charging for them would hand the comparison to Google's free controls. Treat this split as a hypothesis to test in closed testing: the research found no data on whether one-time purchases or subscriptions convert better in this category. Never monetize with ads or data. Play's Age Signals terms also forbid using its age data for advertising or profiling ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/google/play/age-signals/overview)).
